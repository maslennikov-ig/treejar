# Deep research brief: which sales methodology fits this business

Paste everything below into the research model. It is written about a furniture
business and a sales conversation, not about software. Nothing in the answer
should be about AI, chatbots, prompts or automation — assume the reader is
hiring and training human salespeople, because that is the question. Applying
the conclusion to our assistant is our job afterwards, and a recommendation
shaped around AI would bias it.

---

## The question

We sell office furniture in the UAE and we want to choose a sales methodology —
or a deliberate combination of a few — for our inbound sales conversations. We
want a recommendation grounded in what actually fits this business, its channel
and its customers, not a survey of famous frameworks.

## The business

- Office furniture supplier based in Dubai, selling across the UAE.
- We quote from our own catalogue of about 920 SKUs: task and executive chairs,
  desks, four- and six-person workstations, meeting tables, storage, acoustic
  pods. Prices in AED, roughly 140 to 950 AED for a chair and 1,800 to 2,500 AED
  for a four-person workstation.
- We hold real stock and confirm availability per SKU, so lead time and "is it
  actually in stock" are live parts of the conversation.
- We deliver and install.
- The commercial outcome we want from a conversation is a quotation, then an
  order. Our own internal guideline states the task as "understand the
  customer's need and produce a quotation in the shortest possible time".
- We do not discount as a matter of course. We can bundle a multi-item order as
  a package at a combined price, but a discount needs management approval, so
  any methodology whose engine is price concession is a poor fit.

## The channel, which is unusual and matters

Every one of these conversations happens on **WhatsApp**, in text, asynchronous,
with the customer on a phone.

- There is no call, no meeting, no deck, no screen share.
- The customer can vanish between messages and often does.
- Replies are short. Long structured messages do not get read.

## What our customers actually do — measured, not assumed

Counted over 76 real customer conversations in our production database, test
traffic excluded:

| | |
|---|---|
| Median customer messages in a whole conversation | **2** |
| Conversations with only one customer message ever | 27 of 75 (36%) |
| Opening message is a bare greeting or emoji, nothing else | **25 of 74 (34%)** |
| Opening message contains no product word at all | 20 of 74 (27%) |
| Median opening message length | **53 characters** |
| Opening messages over 150 characters | 5 of 74 (7%) |

What they talk about, by conversation:

| topic | conversations |
|---|---|
| price / how much / quotation | 14 |
| delivery, installation, assembly | 12 |
| stock and availability | 3 |
| catalogue, options, "what do you have" | 2 |
| bulk, wholesale, MOQ, discount | 2 |

Who they are:

- **34 of 76 conversations carry clear business signals** — an LLC, an office, a
  team, a headcount, a company name.
- **0 of 76 carry consumer signals** — no "for my home", no apartment, no villa.
- The remaining 41 are simply too short to tell, which is itself the finding.
- Order sizes where a quantity is mentioned: **median 7 units, 90th percentile
  101 units.** Small orders with a long tail of large ones.
- Almost all write English; a small number write Arabic.

## What we want from the research

1. **A recommendation, ranked.** Which methodology should be the spine of the
   conversation, and which one or two should be grafted onto it and for what.
   Name them and say why *this* business rather than in general.
2. **What each one costs us here.** Every methodology assumes something —
   discovery time, a decision-maker on a call, a long cycle, permission to
   challenge the customer's view. Say plainly which assumptions our channel
   breaks, and what a method degrades into when its assumption fails. We are
   especially interested in what happens to question-ladder methods when the
   median conversation is two messages.
3. **The order of operations in the first two messages.** Given a third of
   customers open with "Hi" and may never send a third message, what should the
   salesperson's first reply do, and in what order? Is qualification before
   value a mistake here?
4. **Which of our two customer shapes wins.** Small orders around 7 units and
   occasional 100-unit projects need different handling. Should one method serve
   both, or should the conversation fork, and on what observable signal?
5. **The role of price and stock.** Price and delivery are what customers
   actually ask about, and we can answer both truthfully within seconds. Do the
   methodologies you recommend treat an early direct price answer as a mistake?
   If so, is that advice right for a channel where a non-answer loses the
   customer?
6. **What good looks like, concretely.** For the two or three you recommend,
   give the specific moves a salesperson makes on a WhatsApp thread — the actual
   shape of a reply, not the theory. Short examples in English are welcome.
7. **How to tell use from imitation.** Methodologies are easy to perform and
   hard to apply. Give observable markers that separate a salesperson genuinely
   using the method from one going through its motions.

## Constraints on the answer

- Do not produce a catalogue of every named methodology. We want a decision.
- Where evidence exists on effectiveness in transactional or messaging-based
  B2B sales, cite it and say how strong it is. Where the honest answer is
  "practitioner consensus, no good evidence", say that instead of dressing it up.
- Anything that depends on discounting is out of scope for us.
- If your recommendation is that a well-known methodology is a bad fit here,
  say so directly — that is as useful to us as a positive recommendation.
- Assume the reader is smart and has no patience for filler.

## Useful context on what we already do

Our internal quality checklist scores conversations on fifteen rules: greeting
and introduction, asking how to address the customer, tone and active listening,
genuine interest, a sincere compliment, briefly stating our value, clarifying
questions, the "drill and hole" principle (the customer buys the hole, not the
drill), a comprehensive solution beyond the initial request, an incentive on a
comprehensive order, collecting contact details, establishing what the
customer's company does, confirming the order and next step, and agreeing a next
contact when the customer is not ready.

We would like your honest view on whether that checklist is the right target for
a two-message WhatsApp conversation, and which of its fifteen rules are worth
enforcing in this channel versus which are artefacts of a longer, spoken sale.
